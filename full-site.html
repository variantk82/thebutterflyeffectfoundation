What we are here to do

Imaging should not depend on how far a patient can travel.

Mission

We place handheld ultrasound probes — and the training to use them — with clinicians serving communities that have never had access to medical imaging, so patients can be examined, diagnosed, and directed to the right level of care without leaving home.

Vision

A world where medical imaging is as ordinary as a stethoscope: where any clinician, in any community, can look inside a patient at the moment the question is asked — and where the odds of an early diagnosis are no longer set by the distance to the nearest hospital.

The technology

What the Butterfly ultrasound is

A conventional ultrasound machine is a cart. It costs as much as a car, needs mains power and a dedicated room, and carries a set of separate probes — one shaped for the abdomen, one for shallow vessels, one for the heart. That machine is the reason imaging clusters in cities.

The Butterfly iQ replaces the cart's piezoelectric crystals with roughly 9,000 micro-machined transducers etched onto a single silicon chip — the same kind of semiconductor process that made computers small and cheap. Because the beam is steered electronically rather than by the physical shape of a crystal, one probe does the work of the whole set: deep abdominal views, shallow vascular views, and cardiac views from a single head.

It fits in a coat pocket, draws its power from a phone, and displays the scan on that phone's screen. A clinician can carry it to a village clinic, a refugee settlement, or a patient's home — and answer the question in front of them: is this pneumonia or heart failure, is this pregnancy where it should be, is this abdomen bleeding, does this person need a four-hour ride to a hospital or a course of antibiotics and a follow-up.

Who started this

Meet Isabelle

Isabelle is a 10th grader who started The Butterfly Effect Foundation after learning how much of the world's medicine is practiced blind. Outside of school she [hobbies — e.g. runs cross-country, plays cello, volunteers at …], and she's drawn to [interests — e.g. biology, global health, engineering].

What caught her attention wasn't the technology on its own. It was the gap it exposes. A clinician in a well-resourced hospital can order an image and have an answer in an hour. A clinician in a rural clinic, a shelter, or a mobile unit examines the same patient with the same hands and the same stethoscope — and then has to guess, or send the patient on a journey that many people cannot make.

Isabelle's conviction is that the most valuable thing a portable probe buys an underserved community is not a diagnosis in the abstract — it's triage. Knowing which patient must be moved tonight and which one can be safely treated and watched. Imaging at the bedside turns a guess into a decision, and that decision is what determines whether care arrives in time.

She founded The Butterfly Effect Foundation to close that gap one probe at a time, and to make the case — to her school, her community, and anyone who will listen — that a diagnostic tool small enough to carry should not stop at the city limits.

Where the idea came from

The talk that made the case

TEDMED · Dr. John Martin "What if medical imaging was as accessible as a stethoscope?" Watch on YouTube

He tested the prototype on himself.

Dr. John Martin, a vascular surgeon and Butterfly Network's chief medical officer, gave the presentation that put chip-based ultrasound in front of the public. His subject was access: the ultrasound-on-a-chip transducer pioneered by founder Dr. Jonathan Rothberg and co-founder Nevada Sanchez, and what it means to shrink a hospital's imaging suite into something that plugs into a phone.

Then the talk turned personal. Martin had felt a thickening in his own neck. He put the prototype to his throat and saw a dark mass on the screen — metastatic tongue cancer, found early enough to be treated. The device that was meant to demonstrate a point about access had just made the point itself.

That is the argument this organization is built on. Not that imaging is a luxury worth extending, but that a person's chance of being seen in time currently depends on where they happen to be standing.

What a placement includes
A probe alone is not a program.

Each placement is a device, hands-on training for at least two clinicians at the site, a written scanning protocol matched to the clinic's most common questions, and a check-in at 30, 90, and 180 days.
